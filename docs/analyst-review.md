# NestWell: a worthwhile problem, a business still to validate

A candid assessment of an AI-enabled maternal health companion, with a narrower launch strategy, competitive evidence, and a practical validation plan. (Independent analyst review of the founders' business case, prepared for Shahab. Research checked September 16, 2026.)

## Overview

**Recommendation: fund a focused validation effort before a broad app build.**

The maternal care gap is credible. The proposed combination of AI, personalization, multiple specialties, and support between appointments overlaps substantially with existing products. The strongest initial hypothesis is a postpartum follow-up service connected to a patient's existing OB practice, with a measurable improvement in completed care and manageable staff workload.

**Seller baseline: supplied concept, not a launched or validated product.**

NestWell would support women through preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum, combining maternal medicine, internal medicine, psychiatry, and therapy. AI would personalize education, tracking, and escalation using clinician-designed pathways. No working product, contracted practice, patient usage, clinical validation, pricing, or founder operating commitments were supplied. These are unknown, rather than assumed absent.

- **Clinical need** — Strong basis for investigation. Continuous care and the transition from maternity care to ongoing care are recognized needs.
- **Differentiation** — Weak in the current pitch. Being mother-focused, multidisciplinary, and AI-enabled does not establish a unique position.
- **Commercial case** — Unproven. The key test is who pays, why they switch, and whether delivery costs fit the price.

### What the evidence supports

ACOG recommends an ongoing postpartum care process, with initial contact within three weeks and a comprehensive visit by twelve weeks. Its mental health guidance also emphasizes systems for assessment, treatment, and follow-up after screening. NestWell's continuity concept fits this direction. It should help a patient complete care, not merely identify a need. (ACOG postpartum guidance; ACOG screening guidance.)

A randomized trial of 206 women with pregnancy-related hypertension found that text-based follow-up obtained a blood pressure reading within ten days in 92.2% of the intervention group versus 43.7% with office follow-up. This supports a specific remote-care workflow. It does not establish that a general AI companion improves outcomes. (Hirshberg et al., randomized trial.)

A postpartum chatbot trial randomized 192 women; 152 completed six-week follow-up. Results were mixed: one depression measure improved more with the chatbot, while the other depression measure and anxiety scores did not differ. Participants' average baseline scores were below screening thresholds. This is evidence of feasibility and some potential benefit, not proof of an autonomous clinical system. (Suharwardy et al., published research paper.)

The U.S. recorded approximately 3.6 million births in 2025, according to provisional CDC data. That establishes substantial annual need, but births are not paying customers and do not define NestWell's reachable market. (CDC provisional birth data.)

### Where the timing argument needs adjustment

AI can make information retrieval, language adaptation, documentation, and follow-up more convenient. It can also reduce competitors' development costs. Timeliness is a reason to test now, not a durable advantage by itself. A defensible business would accumulate trusted distribution, reliable workflows, evidence of benefit, and relationships that help patients actually obtain care.

### What this changes

NestWell should not claim to be the first integrated maternal companion or argue that existing competitors provide only static content. The credible positioning is a testable service promise: **women complete the next appropriate step in their care, and the practice can verify that it happened.**

There is also a build-versus-buy question. If the founders primarily want better care for their own patients, adapting or partnering with an existing service might achieve that sooner. A startup makes sense if discovery reveals a repeated gap that incumbent products cannot economically or practically address.

## Guidance

### 1. Start with a defined postpartum service

My preferred first test is enrollment late in pregnancy or before discharge, followed by twelve weeks of coordinated postpartum support. Choose one state, one or two practices, and one operational priority. A reasonable starting priority is completion of postpartum follow-up and mental health referrals. If a partner has a mature hypertension service and monitoring capacity, blood-pressure follow-up is another option. Higher acuity increases staffing and safety requirements.

The first twelve weeks are an evaluation window, not a claim that risk ends there. Provide a planned transition to primary care and mental health care, with later follow-up as the service proves sustainable. Defer preconception and comprehensive pregnancy management until the first workflow earns repeat purchases.

### 2. Make the useful output an action

- **A brief maternal check-in:** a few relevant questions about recovery, mood, symptoms, and practical barriers, with an easy way to skip or request help.
- **A care plan she can understand:** clinician-approved explanations of her existing plan, reminders, and preparation for appointments.
- **A real connection to help:** a named practice contact, correct after-hours instructions, and human review when required.
- **Confirmed follow-through:** record whether a callback occurred or a referral appointment was completed, rather than marking a referral successful when a link was sent.
- **A concise clinician summary:** relevant changes and unresolved issues, delivered through an agreed channel with clear ownership.

Example: when a patient reports persistent difficulty coping, the service can collect a clinician-approved screen, route it to the responsible professional, help find an available covered appointment, and confirm the connection. The valuable result is timely access to appropriate care. Automated screening is not a diagnosis, and the AI should not become the treating therapist.

### 3. Use AI selectively, with clinical accountability

Use AI initially to explain approved material, assemble draft summaries for review, and help organize patient questions. For any symptom or urgency workflow, require clinical design, regulatory review, documented escalation rules, and independent validation. A language model must not override an emergency instruction or reassure a patient merely because an answer sounds plausible. Rules-based logic also needs testing; it is not automatically safe or exempt from regulation.

Define who monitors each queue, their coverage hours, how receipt is acknowledged, what happens if nobody answers, and how urgent users obtain help without waiting for an app response. Budget for that work. Having clinicians on an advisory board does not provide a staffed response service.

Keep mental health integrated, while preserving patient control over sharing. Build paths for pregnancy loss, stillbirth, trauma, and changing preferences so the product does not continue inappropriate pregnancy celebrations or reminders. Avoid excessive tracking that makes an exhausted mother feel she has another job.

### 4. Choose the buyer before expanding features

| Route | Why it could work | What must be proven | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| OB practice or group | Existing trust and a direct introduction to patients | Who controls the budget; total staff time; whether the practice renews | Best first validation channel if founder access is real |
| Hospital or health plan | Greater ability to benefit from better continuity and reduced complications | Procurement, outcomes evidence, implementation, and contract economics | Potential scale route after pilot evidence |
| Employer | A benefits budget and defined population | Why add another benefit alongside established vendors | Selective opportunity, not the default entry |
| Consumer subscription | Fast price testing and direct user feedback | Acquisition cost, paid retention, clinical service cost, finite use period | Secondary experiment unless founders already have distribution |

A referral channel and a payer are different. An OB may gladly recommend the app without being willing or able to buy it. Measure this distinction. Likewise, savings from an avoided hospital visit do not automatically accrue to the practice buying the software.

For a first contract, I would test a fixed pilot fee or a price per enrolled postpartum episode, with defined support limits and separate clinical service arrangements. Do not assume app messaging qualifies for reimbursable monitoring, behavioral-health, or care-management codes. Coverage and billing need payer-specific confirmation.

**Illustrative economics only, not market prices or a forecast.** At $20 per month for six paid months, a consumer produces $120 of revenue before acquisition, support, and operating costs. At an assumed $200 per completed enrollment, 1,000 annual episodes produce $200,000; 15,000 produce $3 million. These numbers explain the volume challenge. Whether either price is sellable is unknown.

For a care-supported episode, 90 minutes of staff time at an assumed fully loaded $60 per hour costs $90 before technology, supervision, coverage, insurance, and overhead. Model high-use patients and after-hours coverage separately. Founder time must count even when initially unpaid.

### 5. Resolve the product boundaries early

**Patient-specific triage:** FDA's January 2026 clinical decision support guidance distinguishes non-device functions from regulated device functions. The clinician-facing exemption cannot simply be assumed to cover personalized patient-facing clinical recommendations. Obtain a function-by-function assessment before building the central symptom and urgency features. An educational disclaimer does not decide the classification. (FDA guidance.)

**Illinois mental health services:** if Illinois is a launch market, the Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act, effective August 1, 2025, is directly relevant. Illinois restricts AI use in therapy and therapeutic decision-making, while allowing specified support uses. Have counsel review the exact conversational features, supervision, and claims. Do not assume an AI-designed-by-psychiatrists product falls outside these restrictions. (Illinois regulator; 225 ILCS 155.)

**Privacy and clinical service delivery:** HIPAA applicability depends on the operating relationship; a practice-contracted app may be a business associate. Consumer apps outside HIPAA can still face FTC health-data obligations. Clinical delivery also requires state-specific licensure analysis. These affect contracts and staffing, not just the privacy policy. (HHS app guidance; FTC guidance; HHS licensure guidance.)

My design recommendation is minimal collection, no advertising trackers in sensitive workflows, clear retention and deletion controls, appropriate vendor contracts, and no model training on patient data by default. Seek explicit operational and legal decisions on record access, mental health information sharing, liability insurance, and incident handling.

### 6. Treat policy momentum as a route to partnerships

CMS's Transforming Maternal Health model supports integrated maternal care through participating state Medicaid agencies. Illinois is among the participants; Indiana is not on the published list. The program runs from 2025 through 2034. This suggests a potential partnership conversation if the launch geography fits. It does not supply an automatic NestWell reimbursement stream or startup grant. (CMS TMaH model.)

### 7. Validate in stages before committing substantial capital

| Stage | Proposed work | Decision evidence |
|---|---|---|
| First 30 days | Interview about 20 women and 8–10 practice stakeholders, including nurses and the budget owner. Review existing alternatives with practices. **Use synthetic examples for a prototype.** | A repeated unmet problem, a reachable cohort, a responsible clinical owner, and a buyer willing to discuss a paid pilot |
| Pilot preparation | Resolve scope, privacy, clinical review, response coverage, referral capacity, and measurement. Establish a baseline and a priced pilot agreement. | The service can be delivered responsibly at a cost the buyer could sustain |
| 12-week patient follow-up | Enroll approximately 50–100 patients across one or two practices, beginning in small batches. Follow each cohort for twelve weeks. | Feasibility, action completion, workload, engagement, and early willingness to renew |
| Expansion decision | Review cases, missed contacts, costs, and patient feedback. Ask a practice outside the founders' immediate circle to pay. | Evidence that the value extends beyond friendly pilot partners |

These sample sizes and timelines are operating suggestions, not a statistical power calculation. Preparation plus complete twelve-week follow-up will generally take longer than a single ninety-day project, especially with rolling enrollment.

**Measure:** enrollment and completion using all eligible patients as the denominator; completion of planned visits; time from a positive screen to clinician assessment and treatment connection; total staff minutes per patient; escalation appropriateness and missed escalations; patient-reported usefulness; and fully loaded delivery cost. Track dropout and differences by language, insurance, and access barriers.

**Continue if:** women complete useful actions, the practice can handle the workflow, the buyer will renew at a viable price, and review finds no unresolved serious safety failures. **Pause or redesign if:** false reassurance occurs, urgent queues go unowned, referral capacity is absent, total workload rises without offsetting value, or enthusiasm disappears when payment is requested. A small pilot cannot prove prevention of rare serious complications or maternal deaths.

### What to say, clarify, and prove

**Say:** describe a defined postpartum follow-up program, its relationship to the woman's care team, and the practical help it provides. Present improved access and reduced workload as goals until measured.

**Clarify:** who owns the postpartum gap today, which existing product was considered, why it fails locally, who pays, and who takes responsibility for urgent contact.

**Prove:** completed follow-up, reliable handoffs, safe operation, sustainable clinical time, and paid renewal. Avoid first-of-its-kind, better-than-clinician, guaranteed reassurance, and proven-outcomes claims.

### Founder implications

The OB-GYN founders' strongest potential advantages are patient trust, knowledge of real workflows, and access to practices for discovery. Those advantages become valuable only with appropriate institutional permission and committed operating time. Assign a clinical safety owner, an operations owner, and a product/technical owner. Confirm intellectual-property and outside-work obligations before using employer systems, patient information, or staff.

The most consequential unanswered question: can the team secure one practice with a named clinical owner and a budget owner willing to pay for a specific postpartum workflow? That would improve the investment case more than another feature or specialty on the advisory roster.
